Accuracy Without Understanding

Andrea Trevisi · on medicine, AI, and whose understanding counts

In a public hospital in Mthatha, a radiologist read a woman's mammogram as normal. The system disagreed: high risk, recommend a biopsy. When she asked why, her clinician could only say the model had found a pattern he did not fully understand, drawn from data on women across the continent. She agreed to the biopsy out of fear of delaying care rather than any confidence in the machine. It found an early cancer. The system was right. And still she left that room frightened, trusting the process less than when she walked in, because at the moment that mattered most, no one could tell her why.

That scene is one of four case studies Munung and Tangwa (2026) use to make an argument that pushed me out of the technical comfort zones where conversations about responsible AI usually settle. What struck me most was how they framed the harm: not as a failure of accuracy, but as a failure of imagination. A system can be correct and still wrong the person in front of it, because AI so often encodes a particular worldview, usually Western, individualistic and biomedical, and then quietly universalises it. Reading this made me see how easily a tool becomes an agent of epistemic dominance, sidelining ways of knowing that do not fit its data structures. It challenged my own tendency to think of AI as neutral unless proven otherwise.

None of this means the familiar safeguards are worthless. Bias mitigation, transparency and consent matter, and I would not want a system built without them. But they are necessary, not sufficient, and it is tempting to treat the checklist as the whole of ethics when it is only the entrance. The harder work cannot be outsourced to guidelines. It asks what the checklist never reaches: who gets to define health, whose values shape the system, and whose voices are missing from it. I found myself recognising how rarely those questions appear in mainstream AI ethics discussion, despite sitting at the centre of what justice in medicine would actually require.

So the article leaves me with humility rather than a solution, and with a demand more exacting than compliance: an approach grounded in dialogue across cultures, disciplines and worldviews. But humility is the mood, not the mechanism, and a principle no one is answerable for is only a sentiment. If the harm is a failure of imagination, then someone has to be responsible for the imagining, for asking whose understanding a system is built on before it is sent into a moment as intimate as a diagnosis. The accuracy, we have. It is the understanding, and the accountability for supplying it, that is still missing.

Reference

Munung, N.S. and Tangwa, G.B. (2026) 'Data science and AI in medicine and global health: the need for inter-philosophies dialogue, cross-cultural ethics and ecocentricity', South African Medical Journal, 116(6b), e3662. doi: 10.7196/SAMJ.2026.v116i6b.3662.
