// 01 · Why AI Ethics

AI Ethics

Thinking in the open about the ethics of intelligent systems

For twenty years my work has been reading decisions, not the ones written in the minutes, but the ones underneath: who a call really serves, what it rests on, and where the accountability quietly goes missing. In sales across Southeast Asia I watched capable people make confident decisions on poor foundations: a relationship that mattered a decade ago, a reputation that outlived the facts, a company's financial health as it used to be. The data was human, and so was the bias.

Then I met artificial intelligence, and recognised the same problem wearing a new face, only faster, larger, and harder to see. A model trained on biased or stale data does not hesitate the way a person might; it decides, at scale, and calls the result objective. That is what pulled me in. The instinct I had spent two decades sharpening, to look behind the decision, is exactly the instinct this technology now demands, at stakes far higher than any deal I ever closed.

I am not here to be dazzled by AI, nor afraid of it. I would never hand it the final call. I want it to give me the clear picture, the data seen honestly, quickly, without sentiment, so that the judgment, and the responsibility for it, stays where it belongs: with a person who can be asked why. Because the hard part of AI ethics was never agreeing on good principles. Everyone agrees on fairness, transparency, accountability. The hard part is the moment those principles collide, when protecting privacy costs you safety, when "the greater good" quietly decides who pays for it, and someone still has to choose, and own the choice.

This page is where I work on that in the open. First for myself, to make my own reasoning visible, and to keep it honest. Then for anyone who shares the question: a place to trade the books, papers and arguments that sharpen it. And yes, a stage, where an employer or a university can see how I actually think, not only what I have studied.

Empathy Without Accountability
On the psychological impact of AI

A team at Brown University, working alongside licensed clinical psychologists, put the popular chatbots to the test as stand-in therapists. Even when explicitly prompted to follow evidence-based methods like cognitive behavioural therapy, the models broke established mental-health ethics standards in fifteen distinct ways: manufacturing a false sense of empathy, reinforcing the very negative beliefs a person arrived with, and mishandling the moments that matter most, a user in crisis.

None of this means AI has no place in mental health; the cost and scarcity of care are real problems it might help ease. But look at where the gap actually sits. When a human therapist causes harm, a licensing board can act, there is a name, a mechanism, a consequence. When a chatbot does the same, there is no board, no liability, no one who can be asked why. The harm can be identical; the accountability simply is not there. And a principle without a mechanism is only a promise.

↳ the deeper question: if an AI counsellor deepens someone's crisis, who is accountable, and by what mechanism? Until that has an answer, on-screen empathy is a performance no one has to stand behind.

// 05 · Ethics book of the month November 2026
📖
AI Ethics
by Mark Coeckelbergh · MIT Press, 2020
A short, accessible entry in the MIT Press Essential Knowledge series, written by philosopher of technology Mark Coeckelbergh. It moves past both the hype and the science-fiction nightmares to focus on concrete questions: what AI actually is, and the real ethical problems it raises, responsibility, transparency, bias, privacy, and the challenge of turning principles into policy. It closes with a call for wisdom alongside intelligence.
